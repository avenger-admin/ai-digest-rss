Gemini agent teams find faster quantum algorithms, and prove they work

Google DeepMind gave separate teams of Gemini 4 agents some well-known quantum algorithms and asked them to make them cheaper. Every few hours the teams met at a “virtual conference” to trade and argue about ideas. Every improvement had to be proved correct in Lean, the formal theorem prover, before it counted, because circuits this big can’t be tested on today’s hardware.

The targets included fusion energy, chemistry and enzyme simulations. The headline result is a circuit roughly 290× cheaper than the baseline. DeepMind is open-sourcing the Lean library, the problem specs and the verified solutions on GitHub. Coming right after the Lean-certified Riemann proof, it’s quite a week for AI plus formal maths.

Jensen Huang is back as Nvidia’s CEO

Following on from last week’s turmoil, Nvidia’s board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board director, effective immediately. The board says it received input from senior levels of the US government and is confident his return “poses no national security risk.” Colette Kress goes back to being CFO full time.

The independent investigation into reports that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort will carry on. Nvidia has also set up a board committee to oversee export-control and national-security risk, and is hiring a new chief compliance officer.

From the inbox: can an AI scientist admit it isn’t sure?

Import AI highlights a Lawrence Livermore paper with a lovely line: “An agent that reports a confident wrong number is worse than one that reports honest uncertainty.” Their SciResearch-Eval benchmark gives an agent a real lab-style task, measuring a material’s thermal properties, and a simulator that may secretly be wrong. The agent has to design its own experiments and report its answers with 95% confidence ranges.

When the simulator was missing real physics, only the strongest frontier models stayed reliable at default settings. Others got there only with maximum reasoning turned on. Jack Clark’s framing question for the issue: “Could the scientific method be emergent?”

From the inbox: consulting isn’t a “thin wrapper” on intelligence

AI as Normal Technology (Sayash Kapoor with Rod Moshtagi, a former PwC senior associate) tests the idea that AI will wipe out knowledge work, using management consulting as the example. Their argument is that clients pay for more than analysis. They also pay for judgment, coordination, credibility and accountability, and AI is weakest at those.

They also argue that, like business computing and the web before it, AI is creating new consulting work, such as organisational change and cyber defence. The catch is that firms have to adapt: build expertise clients don’t have, work alongside the AI labs, and stay neutral across model providers.

A token-cost note for the Copilot and Claude Code work

Import AI covers a new Huawei kernel-tuning benchmark, AscendGYM, that ran models inside Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode. Opus 5 scored best overall and Claude Code gave the best absolute results, but the authors say Opus 5 was “more token hungry” while GPT-5.6 was “more token efficient”. It’s one benchmark, but it’s a handy reminder that the harness and the model both affect cost as well as quality.
