Gemini 4 Hydrogen: the fast one that works with the smart one

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on Thursday, a quick, cheaper model for everyday coding and agent work at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output, with a 1-million-token context and four thinking levels. Google says it scores 57% on Terminal-Bench 4.1, more than triple Gemini 3.8 Flash.

The fun part is Orchestrator in Google’s Antigravity coding tool: Gemini 4 Carbon plans and untangles the hard bits, Hydrogen writes the edits and runs the tests, and they hand work back and forth mid-task. Google reports near-Carbon success while using 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster. It’s rolling out to AI Ultra subscribers first.

ChatGPT Wallet lets agents shop, with a fingerprint on every buy

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on Friday, built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa. Your ChatGPT “dot” agent can find and check out an item, but you review the merchant, items and total first and approve each purchase with Face ID or a fingerprint.

The agent never sees your real card: the payment network issues a stand-in token for each purchase, and your usual card rewards and dispute protections still apply. It’s US-only for Pro subscribers for now. Interesting timing, the same week United said agents can browse its flights but not book them.

From the inbox: open-source AI without Jensen

In Interconnects, Nathan Lambert calls Jensen Huang’s resignation as Nvidia CEO “a major setback” for US open models. Jensen had been funding open-weight work heavily and, as Lambert puts it, using his clout to head off sloppy regulation, and Lambert expects that money to dry up while Nvidia steadies itself.

His bigger point is that the US still lacks a truly frontier open-model lab. Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Small is the best Western open-weight model in a while, he says, but still well behind China’s Kimi K3.1 Pro, and the movement now needs new champions, perhaps from the big cloud companies.

From the inbox: how does an LLM know which word came first?

Sebastian Raschka’s Ahead of AI has a weekend deep dive on positional embeddings, the trick that tells a transformer where each token sits in a sentence. He walks from GPT-2’s learned positions to RoPE, the method most models use today, with diagrams, a little maths and a small PyTorch implementation.

The final section surveys what 2026’s open-weight models actually do. RoPE is still king and probably will be through 2027, but Inkling has borrowed a learned relative position bias from Google’s older T5, and some models now skip RoPE in certain layers. He warns it’s a technical read and says the next post will be lighter.
