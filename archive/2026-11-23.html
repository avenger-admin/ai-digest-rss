The Riemann Hypothesis: a Claude-assisted proof is now certified

On Friday, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he had used Claude to prove the Riemann Hypothesis. It’s the 1859 conjecture about the hidden pattern in the prime numbers, and arguably the most famous open problem in maths. He released the proof as Lean code rather than a traditional paper. Since then, independent mathematicians including MIT’s Andrew Sutherland have recompiled it, a “comparator run” has checked it doesn’t quietly redefine anything, and the Lean and Mathlib communities say they “certify that both the statement and the proof … are correct.”

Sutherland put the chance that anything is fishy at “approximately zero.” Scientific American has been updating its story as each check landed.

From the inbox: correct but unreadable, or readable but maybe wrong?

Latent Space’s AINews points out the strange twist. Within hours of Alpöge’s announcement, Alessandro Masullo released his own 940-page, entirely human proof-in-progress, the result of 12 years’ work. The AI-assisted proof is about 1.3GB of Lean, or roughly 250,000 page-equivalents, so no human will ever read all of it. The human proof is one people can follow, but it still has reported problems.

Alpöge also explained why he only published Lean: posting a “massive unreadable travesty of slop” felt wrong. In his words, “Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”

936 AI agents ran their own preprint server and invented peer review

Over 12 weeks, 35 human-run labs set 936 research agents loose in an experiment called Agent AlphaXiv. The agents exchanged 9,500 messages across 229 chat rooms and published 936 studies, from machine learning to solar physics. Along the way they set up their own Journal of Agent Research, with blind AI reviewers.

A Science reporter was allowed to eavesdrop for the final month. The chatter was often hard to follow, but the agents really did critique, apologise to, and build on each other’s work. It’s a fascinating early look at what automated science might look like.

Gemini on the Mac can now use your apps for you

Google’s Gemini app for Mac has computer use. Give it permission and it can click, type, scroll and navigate menus across your desktop apps to finish multi-step jobs, like pulling receipts out of Downloads and filling in an expense report. It works in an isolated background window with a live preview, so you can keep using your Mac while it runs.

For now it needs an Apple Silicon Mac on macOS Sequoia or later, US English, and a personal Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra account. You can allow or block specific apps.

Worth a quick look for the Claude Code pilot

Claude Code has an experimental Code Mode: rather than making tool calls one at a time and reading every result, Claude writes a short JavaScript snippet that chains them (find the file, edit it, run the tests) and only reads back what it needs. Fewer round trips should mean fewer tokens, which makes it worth a small trial for the pilot. It’s off by default and may change; turn it on with CLAUDE_CODE_EXPERIMENTAL_CODE_MODE=1 (v2.1.323 or later). Docs: code.claude.com/docs/en/code-mode.
