GPT-6 designs new proteins using only reasoning and code

OpenAI says its GPT-6 models can design new protein shapes and the amino-acid sequences that make them, without calling specialised biology models to generate the designs. An internal research model matched or beat specialised tools on many design tasks, and researchers could steer it in plain English, for example by saying where a protein should attach.

In lab tests with Onava, proteins based on GPT-6.2’s designs showed signs of binding to five targets, and redesigned antibodies kept binding. One caveat from OpenAI: specialised models still helped supply sequences and pick which candidates to test. The full details are in a preprint.

Humanity’s First Exam: can a model get the easy stuff right every time?

Most benchmarks measure the hardest thing a model can do. Google DeepMind’s new Humanity’s First Exam measures something different: whether a model answers simple, everyday questions, and small variations of them, correctly every single time. It has 544 basic text and image prompts across 20 foundational skills. One example asks for the colour of the third letter of each word in a neon sign.

DeepMind says frontier models have improved a lot on it over the past six months. There’s a live leaderboard on Kaggle, and a three-week competition running until 15 December asks people to submit everyday questions that today’s models still get wrong.

Personal AI agents launch in more countries

Meta’s Muse agent is now available in the UK. You give it a task in its app or in WhatsApp and it carries it out. It asks before it sends an email or buys something, can’t see your passwords or card details, and each user gets their own cloud computer to work in. It launches with UK partners including Marks & Spencer, ASOS, Currys and easyJet, and it’s free, with paid plans for more usage.

In China, ByteDance’s Doubao team opened an invite-only beta of Xiaodou, a standalone personal assistant it calls experimental, joining Tencent and Meta in the race to build agents for everyday life.

Anthropic asked 58 companies where AI actually pays off

Anthropic’s economists held 66 interviews with 58 of its enterprise customers, covering nearly 300 uses of AI. Most of the value came from doing more, not from cutting costs. The biggest wins came where companies changed how they work by redesigning processes, merging roles and letting more people own workflows, rather than just adding AI to the old way.

On jobs, they found very few layoffs driven by AI. Many companies said they were hiring less, though, and using fewer contractors, especially offshore ones. Anthropic says that could be an early warning for outsourced business services. The bottlenecks they heard about include experts resisting AI and steps like code review that don’t speed up.

From the inbox: a year of AI writing the code, in engineers’ own words

The Pragmatic Engineer is sharing winners from its essay contest on how building software has changed now that AI writes most of the code. Anton Zaides, an engineering manager at HoneyBook, describes how his team moved from careful two-person reviews to vibe-coded frontends and optional reviews. His output went from about one PR a week to more than 20, but the codebase ended up “full of duplications.”

Other winners cover solo builders (“I’m a 100x engineer now!”) and Granola’s Jacob Bartlett on the new core skills of agentic engineering. Their shared conclusion is that agents can write the code, but engineers still have to understand it. The post also links to 40 more essays.

A note for the Copilot and Claude Code work: review is the new bottleneck

Two of today’s pieces land on the same point. Anthropic’s company interviews found that as coding speeds up, steps like code review become the hold-up. In The Pragmatic Engineer, an engineering manager describes his team going from two reviewers per PR to none, and ending up with a buggy codebase. If you’re writing standards, how review keeps up with AI output is worth a line of its own.
