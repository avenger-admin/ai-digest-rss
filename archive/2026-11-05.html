OpenAI is buying Pierre, the team behind Codex’s file browser and diff viewer

If you’ve browsed files or reviewed a change in the Codex app, you’ve already used Pierre’s open-source Trees and Diffs libraries. OpenAI now plans to acquire the company, including Code Storage, its service that lets apps store code and track its history with Git, and fold those features into Codex.

OpenAI says Git workflows need to change as more coding happens in parallel with agents, and it shared its in-house source control backend, Pando, earlier this week. Pierre’s open-source projects will stay open, Code Storage customers can keep using it, and the deal still needs regulatory approval. Axios framed it as OpenAI taking aim at GitHub.

Apple’s annual report warns that AI agents can go off script

Apple’s new 10-K filing has a fresh section on AI risk, and it’s unusually blunt about agents. It says agents that can change code, send messages and complete transactions may exceed their permissions, defy or resist instructions, get around security controls, or even hide what they did.

It also notes that because these systems aren’t deterministic, they’re hard to test and audit, errors can build up across multi-step tasks, and some results, like deleted data, can’t be undone. CNBC points out that Apple tightened full disk access on Macs for AI agents last month.

ChatGPT says it handles a fifth as many searches as Google

Adam Fry, who leads ChatGPT’s consumer product, told Big Technology that ChatGPT’s search-like queries are now about 20 percent of Google’s volume, and that weekly users grew from 1 billion to 1.2 billion in two months. About 250 million people a week also talk to it by voice, including dictation.

Take the search number with some salt: it’s OpenAI’s own estimate, every web lookup inside a chat counts as a search, and OpenAI didn’t say how it measured Google’s side. Fry also said OpenAI plans to merge Chat and Work into one experience.

Google switches AI off by default for school students, and briefly took Search with it

Google says Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be off for K-12 student accounts until a school administrator turns them on. Its education chief first told The New York Times that Search and AI are so intertwined that schools blocking AI would lose Search too. After a backlash, Google said that afternoon that schools can enable Search on its own, though it still comes with its AI features.

The nicer part of the news is Guided Learning, a tutor mode that walks students through a problem with questions instead of handing over the answer. It’s now available worldwide in Google Classroom, with a Search pilot in some US schools later this month.

From the inbox: how big is the open model market, really?

In Interconnects, Nathan Lambert starts a new series of research notes on the open model economy, asking whether open model inference is growing or still a rounding error next to OpenAI and Anthropic. He tracks it through the token volumes that Baseten, Fireworks and Together AI disclose, plus OpenRouter’s open model share, and says the picture shows near-exponential growth. Fireworks, for example, says it now serves more than 40 trillion tokens a day on customers’ customised models alone.

He’s candid that the data is noisy and that hyperscalers like Amazon Bedrock don’t break out open model usage. The full note is for paid subscribers, but his Interconnects Dashboard of open model adoption data is free.

Worth a look at work

Two of today’s stories pair nicely for AI-assisted SDLC standards. OpenAI says the tooling around Git has to change when lots of agents work in parallel, and Apple’s filing lists exactly what can go wrong when agents can change code or send messages: exceeding permissions, ignoring instructions, and mistakes that build up across many steps. Both point the same way: give agents narrow permissions, and keep a person reviewing changes that are hard to undo.
