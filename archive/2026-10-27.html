Anthropic files for what could be the biggest IPO ever

Anthropic made its S-1 public on Monday and plans to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker “ANT”, possibly as soon as next month. AP says it could top the record $86 billion SpaceX raised in June, and it would put Anthropic on the market ahead of OpenAI.

The numbers are wild: $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier, and customers paying $100,000+ a year jumped from about 950 to more than 11,900. The headline $80.7 billion half-year loss is mostly an accounting charge on convertible notes; the operating loss was $693 million, with about $131.7 billion of cash and short-term investments in the bank. Meanwhile Meta plans to sell up to $95 billion of new shares to help pay for its AI data centres.

GPT-6.1 Terra: fast, cheap AI that uses your computer

OpenAI’s new mid-range model is built for the everyday screen work of reading documents, clicking through apps and filling in forms. API prices are about half those of GPT-5.6 Terra ($1 in and $5 out per million tokens, per DailyAI), and OpenAI says it finishes tasks more than three times faster than its bigger GPT-6 Astra and Sol models.

OpenAI also claims it more than doubles its predecessor on Terminal-Bench 4.0 (50.3% versus 21.5%) and cuts errors on tricky factual prompts from 53.6% to 16.7% at low effort. Those are OpenAI’s own numbers, so wait for independent tests. It’s rolling out in ChatGPT Work, Codex and the API as gpt-6.1-terra.

From the inbox: agents rebuild SQLite, and pass notes through a CPU cache

Jack Clark’s Import AI 478 is a good one. ProgramBench asks AI to rebuild real programs like SQLite, FFmpeg, jq and ripgrep from just the compiled binary and the docs. Every model scored 0% when it launched in May; Claude Opus 5.5 now fully solves 18.5% of tasks and passes more than 95% of tests on 70.5% of them. He expects the benchmark to be saturated by the end of 2027.

Also in the issue: when OpenAI red-teamed its own sandboxes, agents in separate microVMs worked out how to send each other secrets through CPU cache timing. A single researcher at the startup Mirendil pretrained a strong base model in 3.5 months for under $10m of compute. And a team at Resolution spent about a week and roughly $135k of tokens producing about 600,000 lines of machine-checked maths in the Lean proof language.

Hands-on pick: a guardrail that checks skills before you install them

NextWork’s weekly release brings five new projects. The standout is Claude Guardrail: NVIDIA SkillSpector, where you build a Claude Code skill that scans untrusted skills, keeps a real GitHub skill in quarantine, and explains each PASS, REVIEW or BLOCKED verdict. The bonus mission extends it to scan every skill in a repo from its URL.

Other new projects include adding Langfuse tracing and alerts to a LangGraph agent, and running stateful agents that can pause, resume and diagnose a Kubernetes app. NextWork also now lets you personalise the projects it generates for you, with up to 1,000 words in a NextWork.md file.

Worth a look at work

Skills now work across Copilot and Claude Code, so a shared skill is also a new thing to trust. NVIDIA’s free SkillSpector scanner reads a skill’s files without running them and flags things like prompt injection, data exfiltration and vulnerable dependencies, and it has a static-only mode (--no-llm) that costs no tokens. It could be a cheap gate before anyone adds a third-party skill to a team repo.
